# How Life Works: Study Plan

Nov 2, 2026 · @Dr. Fogout

## Starting point

Don't start with the philosophy, and don't start with Ball. His book is largely an argument against a standard picture: genes as a blueprint, proteins as lock-and-key machines, development as executing a program. Without that picture firmly in hand, you can't see what he's revising or judge whether he's right.

So the first phase builds the conventional textbook view on purpose. Each later theme then sets Ball's revisions against it.

A math background is a real asset in the middle themes. Much of what Ball gestures at is formalized as dynamical systems, networks and information theory.

**Scope:** both the technical biology and Ball's argument about agency, with the biology as the primary emphasis. Starting from scratch in biology. Organized by theme across the book and outside sources. For personal understanding only.

## Thematic structure

Seven themes, taken in order through Theme 2; after that, move between them as curiosity pulls.

1. **Theme 0: The standard picture.** Cells, DNA, RNA, proteins, the central dogma, metabolism. The vocabulary you need before anything else.

   * Alberts et al., _Essential Cell Biology_ (selected chapters, not cover to cover)
   * Nick Lane, _The Vital Question_ (energy, and why cells look the way they do)

2. **Theme 1: What genes actually do.** Gene regulation, transcription factors, non-coding RNA, and why the "genetic program" metaphor breaks down.

   * Ball's chapters on genes and RNA
   * Denis Noble, _Dance to the Tune of Life_
   * Opposing view: Richard Dawkins, _The Selfish Gene_ (the position Ball pushes against)

3. **Theme 2: Molecular fuzziness.** Protein folding, intrinsically disordered proteins, promiscuous binding, biomolecular condensates. Ball's "life isn't a machine" argument at its most concrete.

4. **Theme 3: Cells as decision-makers.** Signaling networks, noise, cell states, the Waddington landscape. The strongest bridge to math: cell fates as attractors in a dynamical system.
   * Uri Alon, _An Introduction to Systems Biology_ (written for quantitative readers; a core text)

5. **Theme 4: Development and form.** Morphogenesis, Turing reaction-diffusion patterns, positional information, Michael Levin's bioelectricity work, organoids.

6. **Theme 5: Evolution, robustness and evolvability.** Why biological systems tolerate perturbation; degeneracy (different parts doing the same job).
   * Andreas Wagner, _Arrival of the Fittest_

7. **Theme 6: Agency, causation, meaning.** Ball's philosophical claims, now resting on everything above, tested against critics.

   * Kevin Mitchell, _Free Agents_
   * Erik Hoel's work on causal emergence
   * Terrence Deacon, _Incomplete Nature_ (dense, optional)

## Using Claude well

* **Keep a running glossary and an open-questions doc** in the Project. A from-scratch learner hits vocabulary walls constantly; a shared glossary keeps terms consistent across sessions.
* **Ask for quizzes or flashcards** at the end of each theme. Retrieval practice does more for retention than a third read.
* **Ask for diagrams** of processes like transcription, signaling cascades or attractor landscapes. Much of this material is spatial.
* **Bring passages that confused you** in your own words or notes, to work through against the wider literature.
* **First session:** start Theme 0 with a short diagnostic, to find the real starting point after two readings and avoid re-covering what's already absorbed.
